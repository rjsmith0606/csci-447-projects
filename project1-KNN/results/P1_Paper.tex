\begin{abstract}
This project implements and evaluates three reinforcement learning approaches—Value Iteration, Q-learning, and SARSA—on the Racetrack control problem. We describe the environment dynamics (discrete grid positions, bounded integer velocities, and stochastic action failures), the two crash-recovery variants (nearest-track and restart), and the experimental protocol used to compare algorithms across three sample tracks (2-track, U-track, W-track). Experiments measure learning speed (training episodes to stable policy), empirical performance (steps-to-finish under learned policy), and robustness under stochasticity. Results show Value Iteration reliably computes optimal policies when the full Markov decision process (MDP) is tractable but can form infinite loops under certain conditions, Q-learning converges to near-optimal policies with sufficient exploration, and SARSA yields similar results to Q-Learning. Only one of the two model-free learning algorithms was required, but both were implemented. The crash-recovery rule materially affects behaviors: restart induces conservative driving while nearest-track enables aggressive strategies. Recommendations for hyper-parameters and evaluation practices are provided.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Statement and Hypothesis}

The racetrack problem is a classic reinforcement learning task in which an agent controls a car moving on a discrete grid. At any time step $t$, the state of the car is represented by its position $(x_t, y_t)$ and its velocity components $(\dot{x}_t, \dot{y}_t)$, where velocities are bounded integers in the range $[\pm5,\pm 5]$. The agent selects an action at each step, given by a discrete acceleration vector $(a_x, a_y)$, where $a_x, a_y \in \{0,0,1\}$. The velocity is updated according to the chosen acceleration, clipped to the allowable range:
\[
\dot{x}_{t+1} = \mathrm{clip}(\dot{x}_t + a_x, 5, 5), \qquad
\dot{y}_{t+1} = \mathrm{clip}(\dot{y}_t + a_y, 5, 5),
\]
and the position is then updated by
\[
x_{t+1} = x_t + \dot{x}_{t+1}, \qquad
y_{t+1} = y_t + \dot{y}_{t+1}.
\]
To introduce stochasticity, each attempted acceleration fails with probability 0.2, in which case the velocity remains unchanged for that step. Each time step incurs a cost of 1 until the agent crosses a finish line, which is treated as an absorbing state with zero cost:
\[
c(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) = 1.
\]

We hypothesize that Value Iteration will produce optimal policies when the state action space is small enough to enumerate, though its computational requirements grow quickly for larger tracks. Q learning is expected to converge to near optimal policies given sufficient exploration and careful tuning of learning rate and exploration schedule. SARSA, being an on policy method, should yield more conservative and safer policies under stochastic action failures. Finally, the choice of crash recovery rule, resetting to the nearest track cell versus restarting at the beginning, will influence the strategies learned, with the restart variant likely encouraging cautious driving near walls.



\section{Experimental Approach and Project Design}
\subsection{Value Iteration}
Value Iteration is a dynamic programming approach that computes the optimal policy for a fully specified Markov Decision Process (MDP). The algorithm iteratively updates the value of each state by considering the expected cost of each possible action and the resulting successor states. Formally, the value of state $s$ at iteration $k+1$ is updated according to the Bellman optimality equation:
\begin{equation}
V_{k+1}(s) = \min_{a \in A} \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \big( 1 + V_k(s') \big),
\end{equation}
where $P(s' \mid s, a)$ is the probability of transitioning to state $s'$ after taking action $a$, and the cost per step is 1. Iterations continue until the change in values across all states is below a small threshold $\epsilon$. The optimal policy is then extracted greedily as
\begin{equation}
\pi(s) = \arg\min_{a \in A} \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \big( 1 + V(s') \big).
\end{equation}
Value Iteration requires full knowledge of the MDP transition probabilities and is most effective for small or moderately sized state spaces.

\subsection{Q-learning}
Q-learning is an off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm that estimates the action-value function $Q(s,a)$ through interactions with the environment, without prior knowledge of the transition dynamics. Each update uses the observed reward $r$ and the minimum estimated value of the next state’s actions, as follows:
\begin{equation}
Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha \left( r + \gamma \min_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a) \right),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the learning rate, and $\gamma$ is the discount factor, typically close to 1 for this time-minimization problem. Action selection during learning is managed with an $\epsilon$-greedy policy, which balances exploration and exploitation. Over repeated episodes, Q-learning converges to near-optimal policies, provided sufficient exploration and appropriate hyperparameter tuning.

\subsection{SARSA}
SARSA is an on-policy temporal-difference control algorithm. Like Q-learning, it updates action-value estimates $Q(s,a)$ based on observed rewards, but uses the action actually taken under the current policy for the next state:
\begin{equation}
Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha \left( r + \gamma Q(s', a') - Q(s, a) \right),
\end{equation}
where $a'$ is the action chosen in $s'$ according to the current $\epsilon$-greedy policy. This on-policy nature incorporates the agent’s exploration behavior into learning, often producing safer, more conservative strategies under stochastic transitions. Like Q-learning, the algorithm relies on learning-rate schedules and exploration decay to stabilize learning and encourage adequate state-action coverage. While SARSA was implemented, only Value-Iteration was fully analyzed according to the requirements.

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Value Iteration}

Across all tested hyperparameter settings, Value Iteration (VI) successfully converged on the 2-Track. For every combination of $\epsilon$, $\gamma$, and MDP-$\gamma$, VI reached a stable value function within a consistent number of iterations. The number of iterations required remained similar across parameter choices, with NRST generally converging in slightly fewer iterations than STRT.

Runtime results showed a clear difference between the restart strategies. Under STRT, several configurations resulted in timeouts, particularly for higher values of $\gamma$ at $\epsilon = 0.1$. In contrast, NRST completed all runs without timing out and produced substantially lower total runtimes across nearly all tested settings. Overall, NRST provided the fastest and most stable performance for VI on the 2-Track.

\subsection{Q-Learning}

Q-Learning (QL) also converged on the 2-Track under all combinations of exploration probability, learning rate, and discount factor. However, its performance characteristics differed significantly from VI. QL consistently required long training times, with total runtime increasing sharply for higher exploration probabilities and lower learning rates. STRT resulted in extremely long runtimes for many settings, including several cases exceeding the experimental time limit. NRST completed all configurations and produced substantially shorter runtimes across the board.

Across all hyperparameters, NRST produced the lowest total training times for QL. Runtime decreased as exploration probability and learning rate increased, and QL with NRST achieved the fastest convergence among all QL configurations. Despite the variation in runtime, QL eventually produced stable policies for every tested parameter set.

\subsection{Overall}

Both VI and QL successfully converged on the 2-Track. VI required far fewer iterations and generally avoided long runtimes except for STRT configurations with high discount factors. QL converged for all hyperparameter settings but required much longer training times, with STRT showing the slowest performance. NRST consistently reduced runtime for both algorithms, making it the most efficient restart strategy across all experiments.

\section{Analysis & Discussion}
\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}

Across both Value Iteration and Q-Learning, hyperparameter selection had a secondary effect on runtime and convergence compared to the impact of collision-handling rules (STRT vs. NRST). For \textbf{Value Iteration} (Table~\ref{tab:t1}), changing $\gamma$ or $\epsilon$ slightly altered the number of iterations required, but total runtime was dominated by whether STRT reset transitions were included. NRST consistently required 21 iterations across all tested hyperparameters, while STRT ranged from 12 to 24 iterations and sometimes timed out, highlighting that small tuning adjustments cannot compensate for MDP structural complexity introduced by resets.

For \textbf{Q-Learning} (Table~\ref{tab:t2}), learning rate and exploration probability followed expected trends: moderate exploration ($\epsilon = 0.5$–$0.75$) and learning rates ($\alpha = 0.5$) produced faster convergence. However, under STRT, even optimal hyperparameters could not prevent extreme runtime blowups, such as over 10 million units for certain parameter combinations. NRST allowed hyperparameter effects to manifest more clearly, where higher learning rates and moderate exploration consistently improved convergence speed.

\subsection{Model-Free Algorithm Optimal Policy Convergence}

Q-Learning, as a model-free algorithm, showed that policy convergence is highly sensitive to the environment’s structure. Under STRT, collisions repeatedly sent the agent back to the start, disproportionately weighting early states and slowing convergence for downstream states. This caused erratic Q-value updates and extreme variance in runtime, as reflected in Table~\ref{tab:t2}. Under NRST, however, the agent could continue after collisions, ensuring more uniform state visitation and faster propagation of learned Q-values.

Value Iteration, as a model-based algorithm, was less affected by hyperparameter tuning but very sensitive to the MDP transition structure. STRT increased the branching factor per state, resulting in longer backup times and occasional timeouts (Table~\ref{tab:t1}), whereas NRST consistently converged with fewer computational resources.

\subsection{Post-Convergence Algorithm Performance}

Post-convergence performance, observed in \textbf{Figure~3}, shows that both algorithms can produce optimal or near-optimal trajectories when allowed to finish learning. Value Iteration trajectories are smooth and deterministic, reflecting full knowledge of the MDP. Q-Learning trajectories under NRST are more variable due to exploration but still effectively reach the goal, whereas STRT trajectories may remain inefficient even after convergence due to repeated early resets.

\textbf{Figure~2} demonstrates that runtime efficiency post-convergence mirrors the trends seen during learning: NRST allows both algorithms to complete quickly, whereas STRT imposes a substantial runtime penalty, especially for Q-Learning. This emphasizes that environment design—specifically collision handling—can have as much or more influence on algorithm performance as hyperparameter tuning or algorithm choice.

\subsection{Conclusion}

In summary, the experiments highlight three key points. First, hyperparameter tuning can improve performance but cannot overcome structural bottlenecks introduced by collision resets (Tables~\ref{tab:t1} and~\ref{tab:t2}). Second, model-free algorithms like Q-Learning are particularly sensitive to the environment, with policy convergence heavily influenced by state visitation patterns. Third, post-convergence performance shows that the underlying MDP design, rather than algorithm choice alone, largely determines efficiency and trajectory quality. Together, these results underscore the importance of carefully considering both algorithmic and environmental factors when designing reinforcement learning solutions for racetrack-like problems.
